Waterfall

after M. C. Escher’s lithograph of 1961, rebuilt in true isometric 3D

Off Escher’s axis: the illusion is broken

How the water climbs

The camera looks straight down the cube diagonal, the true isometric angle. From there, one step back along both horizontal axes looks exactly the same as one step up, because the two differ only along your line of sight.

All four reaches of the channel run away from you, so the water seems to drift gently downhill. Yet when it reaches the lip it is two and a half storeys higher in the picture, ready to fall onto the wheel again.

What is really there

In three dimensions the aqueduct is an open spiral. Its top end lies 26 units further from you than its bottom end, measured along your line of sight. Three things bridge gaps that exist only in the picture: the waterfall and the lower storey of each tower. Each is cut once, and its upper half is slid back along the line of sight to meet the part it holds up. These are the three Penrose triangles Escher wove the print from.

Press Reveal the trick to turn the camera and see the structure as it really is.

On the towers

On the left tower stands a compound of three cubes. On the right tower stands the first stellation of the rhombic dodecahedron, now known as Escher’s solid.
