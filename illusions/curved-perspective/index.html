Zenith and Nadir

curvilinear perspective, after M. C. Escher’s High and Low (1947) and House of Stairs (1951)

zenith
nadir
horizon

Up and down in one picture

Ordinary perspective projects the scene onto a flat sheet through one eye point. A direction at angle θ from the centre of view lands tan θ away from the centre, so straight up or straight down, at 90°, would land infinitely far away. Even a very wide flat picture can never show the zenith and the nadir.

This picture places every point by its angles. The angle to the right becomes x, and the angle up, turning about the horizontal axis, becomes y. Straight up is +90°, a single point at the top. Straight down is −90°, a single point at the bottom. Every vertical edge in the tower lies on a great circle from the zenith to the nadir, so it bows outwards between those two points. Horizontal lines that run straight across your view stay straight, and all the others bend. The geometry is cut into small pieces and each corner is projected exactly, so the curves are computed, not drawn.

On a flat picture plane a line and its continuation behind the eye meet the plane at the same point. So the vanishing point of all verticals is a single point, and it serves for up and down at once: tilt the view up and that point is the zenith, tilt it down and the same point is the nadir. Escher used this in High and Low. The centre of that print is the zenith of the lower scene and the nadir of the upper one. Curving the picture separates the two, putting the zenith at the top and the nadir at the bottom, as in House of Stairs. Slide towards linear: the verticals straighten into parallels, and the zenith and nadir run off the frame to the same point at infinity.
