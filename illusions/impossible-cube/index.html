The Impossible Cube

front and back under parallel projection, after Necker (1832) and Escher’s Belvedere (1958)

Which edge is in front?

A parallel projection keeps where a point is in the picture and throws away how far away it is. Slide any point along your line of sight and the drawing does not change. Mirror a wire cube in the picture plane and you get a second cube, seen from below instead of from above, with exactly the same twelve lines. That is the Necker cube. Only cues such as shading (we assume light comes from above) and which line hides which decide between the two.

The impossible cube keeps every line and swaps one of those overlaps. Of the two places where a back beam crosses a front beam, one is drawn front over back and the other back over front. Each crossing is believable on its own, but no solid frame has both. To build it, a short piece of the back post is cut out and slid towards you along the line of sight until it passes in front of the top beam.

The belvedere uses the same freedom in true isometric. Seen down the cube diagonal (1, 1, 1), a corner (x, z) of a floor and the mirrored corner (−z, −x) lie on one vertical line of the picture. So the turned upper floor has, in the picture, a corner directly above each corner of the lower one, but at the opposite depth. The pillars that join them lean along the line of sight. From this one angle they look plumb, and the front pillar seems to hold up the back of the floor above.
