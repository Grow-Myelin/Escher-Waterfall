Three Worlds

one surface holds three spaces: what floats on it, what it mirrors, what lies beneath · after Three Worlds (1955)

One surface, three worlds

Light meeting the water splits in two. Part bounces off and shows the world above, here the trees and the sky. The rest passes in, bent towards the vertical by Snell’s law, and shows the world beneath. The leaves lie on the surface itself.

How the light divides depends only on the angle at which your line of sight meets the water. The Fresnel equations give it exactly. Looking straight down, water reflects 2% and you see through it to the fish. At 60° from vertical it still reflects only 6%. Near grazing the share climbs steeply towards 100%, and the pond becomes a mirror. The inset plots this curve, shades the range of angles in the picture, and marks the angle at its centre.

So a single picture shows a gradient. In the foreground you look down steeply into the water, and further off the reflections take over. A dark reflection, such as a trunk, lets the dim world beneath show through. Bright sky drowns it.

Each pixel of the water is computed this way. The rippled surface normal sets the angle. The reflected ray is looked up in an image of the world rendered from the camera mirrored in the water plane. The refracted ray is traced through the water to the carp, the posts or the bottom, losing light along the way.
