Flat to Solid

A drawn creature that becomes three-dimensional, after Escher’s Reptiles (1943)

How it works

The drawing is a tessellation: copies of one lizard, turned by 0°, 120° and 240°, cover the page with no gaps. Each lizard is a hexagon with bent edges. Three alternate corners are centres of three-fold rotation, so each bent edge is the same curve as its neighbour turned by 120° about their shared corner. A bump on one edge, such as a leg or the head, is a dent in the next lizard. Three left front feet meet at one kind of corner and three left hind feet at another.

The solid lizard is built from that exact outline. Its inside is triangulated and every point is raised in proportion to the square root of its distance from the outline, so the trunk rises highest and the toes stay low. Seen from above, its silhouette is the drawn tile, so it fits back into its hole exactly.

The change happens in stages. The pencil outline is inked and filled in, light and shade appear while the body rises in low relief, and then it becomes fully solid. To walk, the body is bent along a path over the desk. Every point keeps its distance along and across the spine, and the legs swing about their joints. Over the book, the regular dodecahedron and the dish, the path follows the exact cross-section of each object, with its corners rounded.

Show construction draws the hole, the hexagon it was cut from with its rotation centres, and the route. On the body it draws lines of equal distance from the outline.
