Flat to Solid

A drawn creature that becomes three-dimensional, after Escher’s Reptiles (1943)

How it works

The drawing is a tessellation: copies of one lizard, turned by 0°, 120° and 240°, fill the page with no gaps. Each lizard is a hexagon whose edges have been bent. Three alternate corners are centres of three-fold rotation, so each bent edge is the same curve as its neighbour turned by 120° about their shared corner. A bump on one edge, such as a leg, is a dent in the next lizard. Three heads meet at one kind of corner, three left front feet at another and three left hind feet at the third.

The solid lizard is built from that exact outline. Its inside is triangulated and every point is raised in proportion to the square root of its distance from the outline, so the trunk rises highest and the toes and tail tip stay low. Seen from above, its silhouette is the drawn tile, and it fits back into its hole exactly.

The change happens in stages. First the pencil outline is filled in, then light and shade appear while the body rises in low relief, and finally it is fully solid. To walk, the body is bent along a path over the desk: every point keeps its distance along the spine and across it, and legs swing about their joints. Where it crosses an edge, the path is the section of the book, the dodecahedron or the dish, with the corners rounded.

Show construction draws the hole’s outline and the hexagon it was cut from, with its rotation centres, plus the path walked and lines of equal distance from the outline on the body.
