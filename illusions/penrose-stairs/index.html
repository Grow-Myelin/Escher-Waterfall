The Endless Stair

the Penrose stairs (1958) and Escher’s Ascending and Descending (1960), as a real spiral in true isometric

How it works

The stair on the roof has four flights and forty risers. Walk it anticlockwise and every step goes up; walk it clockwise and every step goes down. Yet you come back to where you started.

The camera looks along the diagonal (1, 1, 1) of a cube, the true isometric angle. A move of d along that line lands on the same point of the drawing. So a step back of d along both x and z looks exactly like a step up of d.

The stair really is a spiral. Each riser is 0.15 high and each tread 0.75 deep. The two flights that come towards you have 14 risers; the two that go away have 6. Going round once, you climb 40 × 0.15 = 6, and you also drift 8 × 0.75 = 6 towards the viewer along both x and z. The top landing is therefore exactly 6·(1, 1, 1) from the bottom one, and the drawing shows them as one landing.

That landing is cut along its diagonal, in a vertical plane that contains the line of sight, so the cut is seen edge-on. The half where the stair begins stays at the foot. The half where it ends sits at the top, inside the courtyard. A figure crossing the cut jumps 6·(1, 1, 1), which you cannot see from this angle. Reveal the trick turns the camera off the axis and marks the jump with red dashed lines.
