Figure and Ground

One tiling read two ways, after Escher’s Sky and Water I and Day and Night (1938)

How it works

The whole picture is one tiling: light birds and dark fish, each bird surrounded by four fish and each fish by four birds. A bird’s four edges are free curves; the fish is bounded by the same four curves, each shifted by a step of the repeating pattern. So the two shapes fit together exactly, and the pattern has only translations as symmetries (plane group p1, with two creatures in each repeat).

Whether you see birds or fish depends on which shapes stand out from the space around them. Every tone here is a screen of horizontal lines, as in a woodcut, and the ground darkens toward the top and lightens toward the bottom. Above the band the fish take on exactly the ground’s lines and stop being shapes, so the birds are figures on a dark ground. Below it the birds do the same and the fish are figures. In the band both are still plain tiles, equally weighted, and the eye can settle on either.

Detail is added only where a creature has become a figure: eyes first, then feathers or scales, growing with distance from the band. Escher used the same device: the tile is ambiguous, and the context (tone and detail) decides what it is.

The tiling drifts to the left. Once a creature’s neighbours have dissolved into the ground, nothing holds it in place: the birds climb away and the fish dive away. Show the tiling outlines every tile and puts them back in place.
