Penrose Tiling

two rhombs that cover the plane but never repeat, after Roger Penrose (1974)

Why it never repeats

There are two tiles: a thick rhomb with angles of 72° and 108°, and a thin one with 36° and 144°. All their edges are the same length. Each tile carries two arcs of different kinds: bold and hollow in the lithograph, red and cream in colour. Penrose’s rule is that tiles may meet only where every arc runs on across the edge into an arc of the same kind. Nothing else constrains the pattern.

Deflate shows how the tiling is built. Each rhomb is cut along a diagonal into two triangles (dashed in red). Every triangle is replaced by smaller ones, scaled down by the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618: a thin half becomes one thin and one thick half, and a thick half becomes one thin and two thick halves. The new halves pair up into rhombs that obey the arc rule again.

The rule also forces the reverse. In any tiling that obeys the arcs, the tiles can be halved and regrouped in exactly one way into rhombs φ times larger that obey the same rule, and those into larger ones again, without end. If some shift moved the whole pattern onto itself, it would also move every one of these larger rhombs onto another. Once they are bigger than the shift, each would have to land on a spot overlapping its own, which only it occupies. So no shift works. The tile counts show the same thing: thick rhombs outnumber thin ones by φ to 1, an irrational ratio that no repeating pattern can have.

The zoom uses that hierarchy. Two deflations, enlarged by φ² and turned 36°, give back exactly the picture you started with, so the zoom never has to stop. Each red star marks a centre of five-fold symmetry: the pattern round it is unchanged by a turn of 72°. The larger the star, the further that holds; for the larger ones a fine red circle shows where it stops. Such centres come in every size, but at most one point in a Penrose tiling can have that symmetry all the way out. Here it is the centre of the zoom.
