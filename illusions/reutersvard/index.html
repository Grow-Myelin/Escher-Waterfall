Triangle of Cubes

Oscar Reutersvärd’s impossible triangle of nine cubes (1934), assembled in true isometric

How it works

Each cube in the triangle sits in front of the one before it, all the way round. The last cube is also tucked behind the first. No arrangement of real cubes can do both.

The cubes are real. They are laid along x, then y, then z, three steps per side: from (0,0,0) to (3,0,0) to (3,3,0) to (3,3,2). Each one is a unit nearer the viewer than the one before. In space they form an open spiral, not a triangle.

The camera looks along the cube diagonal (1,1,1). The next cube would sit at (3,3,3) = 3·(1,1,1), which is drawn exactly on top of the cube at the origin, so the picture closes into a triangle. To get the overlaps right at the join, the ninth cube is cut along its diagonal plane y = z. That plane contains the line of sight, so the cut is seen edge-on. The half that touches its real neighbour stays put. The other half is slid back 3·(1,1,1), to just behind the first cube.

In Colour, each cube is tinted by its true depth, from warm (far) to cool (near). Reveal the trick turns the camera and marks the jump of the cut half in red, with a dashed outline where a tenth cube would have to be to close the loop in space.
