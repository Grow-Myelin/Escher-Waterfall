Circle Limit

Infinity inside a circle: a hyperbolic tiling, after Escher’s Circle Limit woodcuts (1958–60)

How it works

The disk is Poincaré’s map of the hyperbolic plane. Every fish here is the same size in that geometry. The map shrinks lengths by a factor of (1 − r²)/2 at distance r from the centre, so the fish look smaller toward the rim. The rim itself is infinitely far away. Straight lines are arcs of circles that meet the rim at right angles.

For each pixel, the shader reflects the point in the sides of one small triangle until it lands inside it. The triangle has angles π/2, π/p and π/q. Two of its sides run through the centre. Reflecting in the third side is an inversion in a circle that meets the rim at right angles. How many reflections it took (odd or even) and where the point lands decide the colour. That is exact at any depth. Show the tiling draws those triangles in black and white, as in Coxeter’s 1957 figure, with the {p,q} polygons in heavy lines.

The fish swim in rings around two kinds of centre: the middle of each p-sided polygon and each corner where q polygons meet. Around each centre is a starfish with as many arms as the turn has steps. The white backbones are the lines through the midpoints of neighbouring edges. They divide the plane into those two kinds of ring and are complete hyperbolic straight lines. Every ring turns the same way, so the fish on either side of a backbone pass in opposite directions, like meshed gears. Turning a ring keeps the pattern’s symmetry: it has every rotation of the tiling but none of its mirrors.

Dragging applies a Möbius transformation that is a hyperbolic translation. It moves the plane without stretching it. When the view drifts too far, it is reset by one of the tiling’s own rotations, which leaves the picture unchanged.
