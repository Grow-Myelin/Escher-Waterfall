The Rooms That Follow You

A reverspective: a relief that sticks out, painted with rooms that go in, after Patrick Hughes

How it works

The board carries five shapes that stick out towards you: a tall truncated pyramid in the middle, two lower ones at the sides and two small ones above. They are painted with a facade whose doorways lead into a long hall, two libraries and two niches. The parts that stick out furthest, the flat tops, carry the furthest things in the picture: the end of the hall and the back walls of the rooms.

As you step sideways, the real tops slide across the board faster than the board itself, as near things do. But the painting says they are far away, and far things should lag behind. Your eye believes the painting, so it can only explain the motion one way: the rooms are turning to follow you.

The relief is computed, not guessed. A perspective transformation centred on the eye, with the board as its fixed plane, slides every point of the painted scene along its own line of sight. Depth behind the board becomes height in front of it, and infinitely far lands 45 cm out. It maps planes to planes, so each wall, floor and ceiling becomes one face of a pyramid, and the painted corners fall on the creases. The 5 m hall becomes a pyramid 32 cm high; the rooms become 15 cm.

The paint is projected, not drawn. The scene is rendered from the design eye, 2.4 m from the board, and cast back onto the relief from the same point. From there every patch of paint is exactly where the picture needs it. Remove the paint shows the plain relief. View from above shows its real shape, with the rooms as they are meant to be seen drawn in red behind the board.
