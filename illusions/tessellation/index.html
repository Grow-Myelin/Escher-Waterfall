Regular Division of the Plane

Bending a hexagon into a lizard, after Escher’s Reptiles (1943) and his notebook on regular division

p3  120° turns about · three kinds of centre · orbifold 333 · Heesch type C3C3C3C3C3C3 · dashed: one lattice cell, three tiles

How it works

Escher filled the plane with creatures by starting from a polygon that already tiles it and changing its edges in pairs: whatever is pushed out of one edge is cut into its partner, so neighbours still fit. Which edges are partners, and how one is carried onto the other, decides how the copies are arranged. Heinrich Heesch listed the 28 ways this can work for a tile with no symmetry of its own.

Here the tile is a regular hexagon. At three alternate corners (the coloured triangles) the two edges that meet are partners: one is the other turned 120° about that corner. Bend the solid edge and its dashed partner bends with it. The other three corners are free but move together. Heesch calls this type C3C3C3C3C3C3.

Each turning corner becomes a point where three copies meet, turned 120° from one another. The pattern’s symmetries are these turns and the translations they generate: the plane group p3 (orbifold 333), with three kinds of rotation centre and no mirrors. Every copy has one of three orientations, so colouring by orientation takes three colours and no two neighbours match. Three heads meet at each red centre, three hind legs at each blue and each green one.

Every copy is the same outline under a different turn and shift, so shared edges coincide point for point. Drag a handle and every copy follows. If you drag the outline across itself, the crossing is ringed in red: copies there would overlap.
