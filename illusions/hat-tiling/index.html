The Hat

one tile that covers the plane but never repeats, after Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss (2023)

One tile, never repeating

The hat is a polygon with 13 corners, made of eight kites cut from a grid of hexagons. Copies of it, turned and sometimes flipped over, cover the whole plane with no gaps and no overlaps. But no arrangement of them ever repeats. It is the first single shape known to force that by its outline alone. David Smith found it in 2022 and proved it with Joseph Myers, Craig Kaplan and Chaim Goodman-Strauss.

The tiling here is built by their substitution rules. Hats come in four small clusters, called H, T, P and F. Each kind of cluster is replaced by a fixed arrangement of clusters, which group into four larger kinds with the same names, and so on. Zooming out shows that hierarchy. Lines are inked more heavily the larger the clusters they separate. The colours show which kind of cluster each hat belongs to, at a level that rises as you zoom out.

The proof turns this round. In any tiling by hats, the hats must group into these clusters in exactly one way, and the clusters into larger ones, at every scale. A tiling that repeated by some shift would have to repeat by it at every level, but eventually the clusters are larger than the shift, which is impossible. No tiling by hats can do without mirror images: in each H cluster, one hat is flipped over. They are highlighted here. In the limit there are φ⁴ ≈ 6.85 ordinary hats for every reflected one.

Spectre shows the sequel by the same authors. Copies of this tile kept the same way round can only tile the plane non-periodically. Bending its edges into S-curves stops mirror images from fitting at all. Here the odd ones out are the spectres turned 30° from the rest, about one in 8.9.
