The Devil’s Fork

the impossible fork, or blivet, widely known from D. H. Schuster (1964), engraved stroke by stroke

How it works

The whole figure hangs on six long, evenly spaced lines. At the left they pair up as the outlines of three round prongs, each ending in an ellipse. At the right the same six lines are the edges of a U-shaped bar of square section seen from above: the top and front of a back bar, the slot, then the top and front of a front bar.

So the same line changes its job along its length. Line 2 starts as the underside of a round prong, where the surface turns away from you, and ends as a fold between a top face and a front face. Lines 3 and 4 outline the middle prong at the left and the slot at the right. The gaps between the prongs become solid faces, and the middle prong becomes empty space.

A line drawing gives no depth, only edges, and the eye reads each edge from what is near it. Near the left end the ellipses say “cylinder”; near the right end the corners say “box”. In the middle, nothing settles it. Slide the card over either half and the rest is a consistent object. The hatching shows the same switch: lines that round the prongs taper out where the flat shading of the bar begins.
